# Strategic review — Passage Authority

**Date:** September 9, 2026
**Prepared for:** Steve
**Prepared by:** Independent review of the repository's own roadmap, GTM, legal, and vendor-risk documents, the live site, and fresh competitive research
**Status:** Advisory only. This is one outside read of internal material, not a product or legal decision.

## Verdict

The product discipline here is genuinely unusual — the "workflow coordination, not legal validity" boundary is held word-for-word across the homepage, security page, sales one-pager, legal packet, vendor-risk packet, and outbound drafts, and the live site matches the internal docs exactly. That is rare and worth protecting. But the business is not 60-90 days from a paying pilot. It is not even started selling. As of today, four days after P1 was marked "prepared," there is no named target account, no identified warm-intro path, and no outreach sent — the entire go-to-market motion described in `SALES-OUTBOUND-LAUNCH-PLAN-2026-09-05.md` is still theoretical. Meanwhile P2 has not gotten smaller since it opened; it has grown every single day (Pennsylvania, the authority-scope catalog, institution policy management, MFA recovery, backups) while the reconciliation streak has been reset to zero twice. And the competitive landscape moved while this was happening: Proof.com now runs a dedicated, well-funded, customer-referenced "credit union POA workflow" product with a named case study (Baxter Credit Union), a documented $110/loan savings claim, and a June 2026 webinar series aimed at exactly this buyer. If Steve does nothing else this month, the highest-leverage move is not another engineering gate — it is naming twenty real institutions, finding the warm intro for five of them, and having the first unscripted discovery conversation. Everything else can happen in parallel.

## 1. Product vision and roadmap sanity check

The P0 → P1 → P2 → P3 gating structure is still logically coherent: close demo readiness, then prepare a pipeline, then earn the right to touch real data, then expand into enterprise identity. That sequencing is sound and the "no-go for real data, no-go for outbound" discipline is the correct posture for a product that touches POA documents and identity evidence. The problem is not the shape of the gates — it's that P2 behaves like a gate that re-opens every time someone looks at it closely. Reading the last five days of roadmap updates in order: September 5 says P1 is ready to launch pending a timed demo rehearsal; September 6 finds two persona bugs and a delivery-tracking gap; September 7 finds Stripe refund double-counting, an out-of-order-webhook defect, and starts a reconciliation clock that immediately blocks; September 8 finds that the institution "policy catalog" buyers will be shown in a demo is a hardcoded two-action fixture, that Pennsylvania's agent Acknowledgment requirement isn't met, and that Supabase Free has zero backups and no point-in-time recovery; September 9's checkpoint still lists eight open blocking items. This is a team (or an unusually rigorous agent) that is good — genuinely good — at finding real problems. But "P2 closes in about two weeks" has now been true, and then false, on four consecutive check-ins. The September 15 floor date in the roadmap is only a floor if nothing else is discovered between now and then, and the last two weeks of history says something else will be discovered.

The bigger sanity-check finding is a mismatch between where engineering effort has gone and where the business actually is. There are 148 passing domain tests, eight replayed Stripe negative-path scenarios (duplicate events, out-of-order refunds, tampered payloads), a real seven-day reconciliation job reconciling Passage's ledger against Stripe and HubSpot, and three-layer privileged MFA with backup-factor enrollment — this is the engineering posture of a company defending against abuse at scale. It has zero paying customers and zero completed discovery calls. Building idempotent billing infrastructure and refund-edge-case correctness before a single institution has agreed to pay is solving next year's problem before this quarter's. None of that work is wasted, but the sequencing inverts the usual order: normally you find out whether anyone will pay $5,000 before you harden the systems that protect a $5,000,000 book of business.

On the "workflow coordination, not legal validity" boundary specifically: I looked for scope creep and did not find meaningful drift. The homepage, security page, sales one-pager, both legal/vendor packets, and the outbound drafts all use the same "does not verify identity / does not notarize / does not decide legal validity / the institution keeps the final decision" language, and the live site matches word-for-word. The one place the team caught itself overclaiming — the website line implying an institution "defines its own evidence requirements" when the product actually ships a fixed three-item checklist — was flagged and is being corrected before it reaches a buyer. That is exactly the right instinct, and it is the strongest evidence in the whole document set that this team can be trusted not to oversell. Keep that discipline; it is a real asset against a competitor like Proof that oversells constantly (see below).

## 2. GTM strategy critique

**The beachhead is still right, but it exists only on paper.** New York community banks and credit unions, reached through warm intros ahead of cold outbound, sponsoring one narrow POA workflow, is a sound wedge — narrow enough to demo cleanly, painful enough (per the CFPB citations already in the selling brief) to get a meeting, and small enough that a $5,000 pilot doesn't need board approval at most institutions. But there is no `P1-TARGET-ACCOUNT-LIST` file anywhere in this repository — I looked under `docs/`, `docs/research/`, and `docs/archive/`, and the legal-review packet independently confirms the same absence. The only artifact that exists is a plan to build a 20-account list, dated September 5, that as of September 9 has not produced a single named institution, a single named buyer, or a single identified warm-intro path. Four days is not a long time in absolute terms, but it is 100% of the time P1 has been "prepared," and in that time zero prospecting has happened. This is the one finding in this review that is purely a matter of doing the work, not resolving ambiguity — Steve doesn't need another gate to close before this can start.

**The $5,000 founding pilot price is probably fine, and possibly a missed opportunity to charge more.** I couldn't find the specific $20,000-$100,000 CU procurement-budget figure referenced in the brief for this review inside the current repo — it isn't in `PRICING-AND-PACKAGING-RESEARCH.md` or the selling brief, so either it lives in a source I couldn't locate or it's from outside this document set. What I can say from fresh research: typical credit union core-banking deals run $180K-$1.5M with 40-60% implementation overhead on top, which puts $5,000 well inside the range a mid-level operations manager can approve without a full vendor-risk committee sign-off at most institutions — that's a real selling advantage, since it lets the pilot skip the procurement gauntlet that kills slow-moving startup deals. The risk isn't that $5,000 is too high; it's that the pricing doc itself admits the annual number is still a complete guess, and Steve is walking into pilot conversations with no tested hypothesis for what year one actually costs. Given how much engineering time went into billing precision, remarkably little has gone into even a rough willingness-to-pay signal. Fix this by asking the pricing question directly in the first five discovery calls, not by waiting for the pilot to finish.

**The "operating tension" positioning is honest and well-sourced, but it is not the same thing as differentiated, and it just got weaker.** The outbound drafts correctly avoid publishing "defensible, not just faster" as an external claim, since the roadmap itself marks that as an unapproved V3 hypothesis — good discipline. Instead they lean on a CFPB-sourced "double bind" framing (act too slowly and you block a legitimate caregiver; act too loosely and you enable elder financial abuse). That framing is true and citable, but it describes the *problem*, not why a bank should pick Passage over the alternative. And the alternative just got a lot more concrete: Proof.com — the eSign/notarization company already selling to credit unions — runs a dedicated "Power of Attorney" use case page, a "Credit Unions" industry page with named CU logos (Baxter, Wright-Patt, BCU, Gesa, Centra, Hughes FCU), a blog post titled "How Credit Unions Can Fix Their POA Workflow" with a real customer quote from Baxter Credit Union's loan-servicing supervisor, a claimed average savings of over $110 per loan versus paper, and a June 2026 webinar called "The Digital POA Playbook." Proof's pitch is materially more complete than Passage's: it verifies identity (IAL2), notarizes remotely, produces what it calls a "tamper-proof audit trail that stands up to legal and regulatory scrutiny," and routes the finished document straight into the credit union's system of record — i.e., it claims to do several of the exact things Passage explicitly and proudly does not do. A credit union operations buyer comparing "one vendor that already handles our loan e-signing, has a named CU reference customer, and quotes a dollar figure" against "an early-stage startup with no customers yet that explicitly does not verify identity or notarize" is very likely to reach for the familiar, more complete-feeling option, especially if that vendor is already inside the building for other document workflows. This doesn't mean Passage's narrower "the institution keeps the decision, we just keep the record straight" wedge is wrong — it may in fact be the more honest and more durable position for exactly the reason the roadmap's own V3 section identifies (a signing/identity vendor extending into the decision layer is the named strategic threat, and that is now observably happening, not hypothetical). But it means the selling brief's competitive table, which still says to "confirm each named competitor's boundary from primary evidence before using this comparison externally," needs to actually be updated now that primary evidence exists, and the pitch needs a sharper answer to "why not just add this to the Proof relationship we already have" — right now there isn't one written down anywhere in the outbound materials.

## 3. The single biggest risk to landing a paying pilot in the next 60-90 days

It's sales-process, not technical and not legal, and I don't think it's close. The legal and technical gates are slow and keep growing, but they are converging on real, fixable problems by people who are being honest about what's missing — that's a process that works, just not a fast one. What isn't converging at all is the pipeline, because there isn't one yet. Zero named institutions, zero warm-intro paths, zero discovery calls, four days after "prepared" status was declared. A 60-90 day pilot clock can't start until a real institution says yes, and a community bank or credit union's own internal approval cycle — even for an informal, sub-$5,000-threshold pilot — realistically eats several weeks between first conversation and signature once you account for a security/vendor-risk look, however lightweight. Given that today is September 9, outbound hasn't started, and P1/P2 haven't formally closed, the honest math is that Steve is already spending down the 60-90 day window before it opens.

The second-order risk, which will surface the moment a real institution's compliance or IT person actually reads the vendor-risk packet Steve would have to hand them, is that the packet is honest about genuinely disqualifying gaps for a vendor about to hold POA documents and photo IDs: no backups of any kind on either live database (Supabase Free plan, no point-in-time recovery), no data-retention or deletion policy, no completed penetration test, no SOC 2 or equivalent. None of this is hidden — the packet says so plainly, which is to the team's credit — but a genuine credit union vendor-risk reviewer, even for a small pilot, is unlikely to wave through a vendor with zero backups of a database holding uploaded identity documents. Backups and a written retention policy are cheap and fast to fix (the roadmap already prices Supabase Pro at roughly $25/month) and should happen before the first serious discovery call reaches a security conversation, not after.

So: the fastest path to a real 60-90 day pilot is not more roadmap hardening. It's (1) naming real institutions and finding real warm intros this week, (2) closing the two or three P2 items that are genuinely one-way doors for handling real customer data — backups and a retention policy chief among them — while treating the rest of P2 as parallelizable with early conversations rather than a precondition for having them, and (3) writing down, before the first live discovery call, the honest one-paragraph answer to "we already use Proof for e-signing and notarization — why do we need this too." Right now none of those three things has an owner or a date attached, and everything else in this review is secondary to that.
